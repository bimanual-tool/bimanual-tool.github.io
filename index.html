Bimanual Dexterous Tool Use

From Contact-Rich Execution to Generalist Robot Intelligence

Half-day Workshop • Austin, Texas, USA • November 9 2026

Call for Papers Program

About the Workshop

Dexterous manipulation has made rapid progress in grasping and pick-and-place, but robots still struggle to use tools with the flexibility and physical competence of humans. Humans not only use general tools across kitchens and household work, but also wield specialized tools for surgery, sports, music, laboratories, repair, and manufacturing. These behaviors are not simply longer versions of pick-and-place: the tool becomes a physical mediator between the body and the world. Successful tool use requires sustained contact, force regulation, evolving hand–tool–object relationships, and often bimanual coordination — where one hand stabilizes, repositions, or constrains the object while the other applies the tool.

Recent progress in dexterous manipulation, imitation and reinforcement learning, tactile sensing, teleoperation, humanoid control, VLA, and world models suggests the field is ready to treat tool use as a central robot learning problem. Yet tool use exposes a fundamental conflict: practical systems require fast, physically grounded, contact-rich execution, while general-purpose systems require high-level reasoning over affordances, future states, and generalization across tools, objects, tasks, and embodiments. Existing systems rarely satisfy both requirements at once.

"How can robot learning bridge physically reliable, contact-rich tool execution with high-level generalizable reasoning?"

Core Challenges & Research Questions

1. Hand–Tool–Object Representations

What representations make the evolving relationship between hands, fingers, tools, objects, contacts, and forces learnable and transferable — graphs, object-centric states, 3D geometry, tactile/force features, language-conditioned abstractions, or hybrids?

2. Bimanual Coordination

Many tool-use tasks require one hand to stabilize, guide, regrasp, or constrain while the other manipulates. How should robots learn role assignment, hand switching, synchronization, and recovery in long-horizon bimanual tool use?

3. Contact-Rich Execution

How can robots execute tool-use skills at real-time speed while maintaining stable contact under uncertainty in force, friction, compliance, deformation, and object motion? Which parts can be learned from data, and which still require explicit planning, feedback control, or physics-based constraints?

4. Generalizable Tool-Use Reasoning

How can world models, video prediction, VLA policies, and language-conditioned planners infer tool affordances, predict physical effects, and generalize across unseen tools, objects, and tasks? Where do they fail when success depends on precise contact rather than semantics alone?

5. Bridging Intent and Dexterity

What is a meaningful interface between generalist models and dexterous controllers? Should high-level systems provide future-state targets, keyframes, contact plans, affordance maps, language plans, object-centric goals, or learned latent actions?

6. Data & Embodiment Transfer

How can robots learn tool use from human videos, teleoperation, motion capture, tactile demonstrations, simulation, and self-practice despite differences in morphology, sensing, compliance, and control frequency? What transfers, and what must be relearned?

7. Evaluation for Bimanual Dexterous Tool Use

Binary task success can hide brittle contact, unsafe forces, unrealistic behavior, or poor generalization. What metrics and benchmarks should capture contact stability, physical plausibility, execution speed, force regulation, robustness, tool/object generalization, and real-world transfer?

Call for Papers

We invite non-archival submissions on tool-mediated physical interaction, including bimanual and single-hand tool use across general, surgical, sports, musical, and household settings; tactile and contact-rich policies; human-to-robot transfer; robot world models; VLA policies; simulation; hardware; and evaluation — as long as the work addresses tool-mediated physical interaction.

Submissions are accepted in three formats: full workshop papers (up to 8 pages), short papers or extended abstracts (up to 4 pages), and demo or position abstracts (1–2 pages), excluding references and appendices. We welcome mature works as well as early-stage ideas, negative results, benchmark proposals, hardware platforms, teleoperation pipelines, and real-world failure cases. Accepted contributions are presented as posters, with a subset selected for oral spotlights and demos.
